One metric, three owners

01
March 2026, one meeting

Three owners report three collection rates for the same month

94.1%
Finance: cash received in March over March's billing
93.8%
Collections: March bills paid within 60 days of due, net of disputes
73.5%
Customer care: share of March bills paid in full by the due date

Each is computed correctly from its own definition. None of them is "the" collection rate yet.

Numbers from Lab 1.1, run on the course data. Ask: which one would the board read as "how much of what we billed did we collect"? Expect a split vote. The point is not who is right: each owner answers a different question with the same name.

Read the definitions, not the numbers

Four choices separate the three definitions

ChoiceFinanceCollectionsCustomer care
Populationcash that arrived in MarchMarch's billsMarch's bills
Windowthe calendar month60 days after dueby the due date
Disputesin the denominatoroutout
Unitvaluevaluecount of bills
And one thing that is not a choice. The finance figure reads the payments table as loaded.

Population is the big one: a cash basis mixes payments for January bills with March bills, so it moves with last quarter's arrears, not with this month's behaviour. The callout sets up the duplicate batch, which participants find in the lab.

A bridge, one change at a time

A walk turns one argument into four separate decisions

Finance as reported
94.1%
Duplicate batch removed
90.2%
Cohort, 60 days, gross
92.9%
Disputes out
93.8%
Window 30 days
86.5%

Each row changes one thing. The council decides each row on its own merit.

From Lab 1.1. The duplicate batch is worth 3.9 points of the finance figure; the window change is worth 7.4 points. Ask which row is a data error (the duplicate) and which rows are definition choices (the other three). A walk is the trainer's best tool against "my number is right": it gives everyone a row to agree or disagree with.

Lab 1.1, step 2

A payment batch loaded twice is a data error, fixed once in the data

SELECT batch_id,
       COUNT(*) - COUNT(DISTINCT (bill_id, paid_date, amount)) AS extra_rows,
       SUM(amount) FILTER (WHERE load_no > 1)                  AS extra_amount
FROM fct_payment
GROUP BY batch_id HAVING extra_rows > 0;
-- B20260317 | 80 | 24802.51
Not in a report formula. Fix it in the payment mart, so every metric built on payments is right at once.

The batch of 17 March 2026 was loaded a second time: 80 extra rows, SAR 24,802.51. In the semantic model of Day 2 the payment dataset deduplicates on bill, day, amount and batch. Ask: why not just subtract it in the finance pack? Because the next metric built on payments would carry it again.

How the council decides

Agree on criteria first; the definition follows from them

  • Comparable month to month: a cohort of bills, not whatever cash arrived
  • Closes in time: before the second month-end close after the due date
  • Owed now: disputed amounts out until the dispute is decided
  • Value, not count: the board manages cash

Certified collection rate

Value paid within 30 days of the due date on the month's bills, over the amount due net of open disputes, deduplicated, reported once the window has closed. March 2026: 86.5%.

The criteria are in data/definitions/collection_rate_three.yaml. Criteria are easier to agree than numbers, because nobody's figure is on the table yet. The owner of the certified metric is Majdi Bawazir (collections); Jood Al-Muhaisen approves. Care keeps its figure under another name: it is a useful measure of customer behaviour, not a collection rate.

A rule that looks like a detail

A cohort metric is not reported until its window has closed

Without the rule

May 2026 bills are due on 24 June; with data to 30 June, almost no 30-day window has closed. The rate looks like a collapse.

With the rule

The metric reports a cohort only when due date plus 30 days is on or before the cut-off. May waits for August.

An assistant will not know this. Unless the layer enforces it, "what was the collection rate last month?" gets a wrong but plausible answer.

This is the first wrong-but-plausible trap of the course; Day 3 measures how often the assistant falls into traps like it. In the model the rule is the flag matured_30 on the collection dataset, used as the metric's filter.

Checkpoint

The meeting wants one number by tomorrow

The chief financial officer asks you to "just average the three collection rates" for the board pack until the council meets. What do you do?

Report the average, 87.1%, with a footnote
Report the walk, and the certified definition's value once the council agrees it
Report the finance figure, since finance owns the board pack
Report the care figure, since it is the most cautious
Why. The three figures differ by definition and one carries a data error; an average hides both. The walk shows each difference as a decision someone can take.

Answer: B. The average of 94.1, 93.8 and 73.5 is about 87.1%: close to the certified 86.5% by luck, which is the danger. If the room picks A, ask what the average would mean to a collector.

data/metrics/collections.yml

A metric file holds the definition and the promise around it

- name: collection_rate
  type: ratio
  numerator: collected_30
  denominator: net_due_30
  owner: Majdi Bawazir
  version: 1.0.0
  status: certified
  description: Share of the net amount due on the period's bills paid
    within 30 days of the due date (certified)
  ai: {synonyms: {ar: [نسبة التحصيل, معدل التحصيل]}}
  tests:
    - reconcile: "SELECT ... FROM fct_payment ... / ... FROM fct_bill"

Three kinds of keys: what the compiler uses (type, numerator, denominator, dataset, agg, expr, filter); what people use (owner, version, status, description); what the assistant uses on Day 3 (the ai block: synonyms and examples). The same file feeds all three, so they cannot drift apart.

Metric types in the course layer

Four types cover the metrics of all four areas

Simple

An aggregate of one dataset, with an optional filter. billed_amount

Ratio

Two metrics, divided after aggregation. collection_rate

Cumulative

A simple metric summed to date in the fiscal year. cash_collected_fytd

Derived

An expression over other metrics. complaints_per_1k_lines

The same four exist, under other names, in MetricFlow (simple, ratio, cumulative, derived) and in Cube's measures.

The course's compiler is labs/qsem.py, about 300 lines over DuckDB: a stand-in for MetricFlow or Cube so the labs run anywhere without a server. Names of tools are examples, not endorsements. Sources: MetricFlow documentation (docs.getdbt.com, about MetricFlow) and Cube's data modeling concepts.

A ratio is divided after aggregation, never averaged across parts

From Lab 1.2. The plain average gives government, 9% of the money, a third of the weight. The average of twelve monthly rates happens to be right (85.64% against 85.65%) only because months are of similar size: that is why the mistake survives in many dashboards. A ratio metric stores numerator and denominator, so every grain divides correctly.

Proof against the source

A reconciliation test compares the metric with SQL that bypasses the layer

→

3 · Equal?to a tolerance
Lab 1.2. Drop the maturity filter from net_due_30 and its test fails: SAR 9,249,163 against 7,935,267.

The test is written by the same person, but against the marts, not through the layer: it catches a lost filter, a wrong join, a compiler change, and it keeps catching them after the author has left. Totals over all data are the minimum; the capstone adds tests by month or by segment where the risk is.

Checkpoint

A regional rate for the scorecard

The regional scorecard needs one collection rate for the year. A colleague computes each month's rate, then averages the twelve. Where does that go wrong first?

When a month has a duplicated payment batch
When the fiscal year starts in April
When months, or the parts averaged, differ much in size
When rates are rounded to one decimal place
Why. An average of ratios weights each part equally. It is close to the ratio of sums only when the parts are of similar size, as Qamar's months are and its segments are not.

Answer: C. Bridge to the lab: participants will see 85.6% against 75.8% on segments.

Session 3

Ownership and change control

  • Distinguish the owner, the steward and the approver of a metric
  • Version a metric change as major, minor or patch
  • Take a change through review rules that run in continuous integration
03
Who does what

An owner answers for the meaning; a steward answers for the file

Owner

A business lead. Approves every change of meaning. Majdi Bawazir for the collection rate.

Steward

The data platform team. Edits the file, runs the tests. Zeyad Al-Juraisy's team.

Requester

Anyone. Opens a change request with the reason and the reports affected.

One owner per metric. Two owners means nobody decides when they disagree.

The NDMO Data Management and Personal Data Protection Standards include a Business Intelligence and Analytics domain (5 controls, 10 specifications) whose plan and use-case controls expect named responsibilities; a catalogue with owners is how an analytics team shows it. Sources: NDMO standards, section 9.9.

Semantic versioning for metrics

The version says whether a reader's number will move

ChangeExampleBump
Meaning: expression, filter, inputsbilled amount without VATmajor 1.0.0 → 2.0.0
New field or statuscertified after tests passminor 0.9.0 → 0.10.0
Words onlyan Arabic synonym, a clearer descriptionpatch 2.0.0 → 2.0.1

A major change keeps the old meaning reachable under a new name until its reports have moved.

Ask: is adding a synonym really a change? Yes for the assistant (Day 3 shows synonyms change answers), but not for the number: a patch. A meaning change to a certified metric is the expensive one: every report that reads it moves with it, without its owner's knowledge, unless the version says so.

labs/ci/check_metrics.py

Six review rules run on every pull request, so memory is not the control

  1. Owner, version, status and description on every metric
  2. A certified simple metric has a test
  3. A changed metric has the right version bump
  4. The changelog names the metric and its new version
  5. The owner approves the new version, and is not its author
  6. Nothing is removed unless it was deprecated first

The script compares the metric files on the branch with those on main using git, and exits 1 on any finding. labs/ci/metrics-ci.yml runs it in GitHub Actions together with the reconciliation tests; participants copy both into the capstone repository.

The human part

A breaking change is announced before it takes effect

Announce

Two monthly closes ahead, to every report owner the lineage finds.

Run both

The old meaning under a new name, deprecated, beside the new version.

Retire

After the sunset date, when no report reads the old one.

CR-07 moves ARPA too. ARPA is derived from the billed amount: its owner must hear about the change even though nobody touched ARPA's file.

Checkpoint

A fix to the maturity rule

The steward finds that net_due_30 counted cohorts one day early and fixes the filter. The certified figure for March moves by 0.1 point. Which version and approval does the change need?

Major version, approved by the owner, with a changelog line
Patch version, since the effect is small
Minor version, approved by the steward who found it
No version, since it corrects a bug
Why. The filter is part of the meaning of a certified metric: every reader's number moves. Small or not, the owner approves and the version says it.

Answer: A. Some teams use a minor bump for corrections; accept that argument only if the team's process says so in writing. What is never acceptable is no version and no owner.

Day 1 · Wrap-up

Three things to keep

Walk, then certify

Split a disagreement into one change per row; fix data errors in the data.

A metric is a file

Definition, owner, version, status and tests together, reviewed by rules in CI.

Lineage finds the reader

Aliases and dependencies, not a text search, tell you who a change will hurt.

Ask each table for the one decision from the walk they would argue about at their own organisation. Collect two or three.
